What would a united Ireland cost the Republic?

This report walks through the fiscal model behind the appendix to A Blueprint for a Unified Island, step by step. Every figure on this page is worked out live by the model as the page opens, and every figure is an illustrative scenario in constant 2023 euros, not a forecast.

1. Where the cost starts

The UK publishes a figure for the gap between what is spent in Northern Ireland and what is raised there. For 2022-23 it comes to in 2023 euros, but that is not the bill a united Ireland would inherit.

Some of that spending is UK defence and central costs that would not move to Dublin, and some receipts raised in Northern Ireland are booked elsewhere. Doyle (2025) adjusts for these, which brings the gap to .

The rest depends on a deal with the UK about pensions and debt interest built up before unity. If the UK keeps paying both, which this report calls the favourable deal, is left.

2. Bringing pay and welfare level

Public pay and welfare are lower in Northern Ireland than in the Republic. Bringing them up to Republic levels is the largest single cost of unity, and the published estimates of it differ widely.

So the model reports four cases and treats none of them as central. The upper case uses the gross gaps from FitzGerald and Morgenroth (2024), and the lower case drops the welfare gap altogether and is a bound rather than an estimate.

Gaps are measured on 2019 pay and welfare bills and converted to 2023 euros. Part of any pay rise comes back as income tax, shown as the share returned.

3. Four ways to unify

The four designs differ in how fast pay and welfare rise, how much extra is spent in the first ten years and how much duplicate administration is kept. The deal with the UK is set separately, so any design can be run on either deal.

4. The results

Each year the model grows Northern Ireland spending and receipts forward and adds the costs of the chosen design. Anything not covered is borrowed at a real interest rate of 2%, and the interest is added to the next year's cost.

5. The three fiscal checks

Three checks were set before any result was run. Total State debt must stay below 80% of GNI* plus Northern Ireland output, the yearly cost must stay at or below 2% of GNI*, and the cost must fall every year from year 20.

The grid shows how many checks each design passes on the favourable deal at four growth rates. The second table shows the steady growth each design needs to repay its borrowing within 25 years, against 1.3% for Northern Ireland since 2000.

7. How robust are the results

This part tests how far the results depend on the assumptions. It uses the paper's starting scenario, Managed Integration in the upper case on the favourable deal at 2% growth, unless a table says otherwise.

Values are assumptions rather than estimates drawn from data, so treat them as test settings only.

7a. Which assumptions matter most Test values

Each assumption is moved between a low and a high value with everything else held still. Northern Ireland growth matters far more than anything else.

7b. Many assumptions at once Test values

Here every assumption from 7a is drawn at random within its range, 1,000 times for each case. The spread shows how sensitive the result is to the ranges, and it is not a probability forecast.

The draws use a fixed seed in this page, so the figures repeat, but they differ slightly from the notebook, which uses its own random numbers.

7c. Which choice explains the spread

Running every mix of case, design, deal and four growth rates gives 128 scenarios. The table splits the variation in year 25 results into the part each choice explains.

7d. Why the debt ratio moves

Each year's change in transition borrowing, as a share of income, splits into the new primary cost, interest on past borrowing and the fall that comes from income growing. With a 2% interest rate and 2% growth, interest and growth cancel out, so the ratio rises only because of new primary cost.

7e. When interest rises with debt Test values

The paper holds the real interest rate at 2% whatever happens to debt. This test adds a premium for each point that total debt sits above 80%.

7f. One number per scenario

Adding up 25 years of primary cost, and discounting later years at 2%, gives one figure per scenario. Shown as a share of discounted GNI*, it is the average yearly cost and can be set against the 2% burden limit.

Interest is left out because it pays for borrowing in earlier years, which are already counted.

7g. The Republic's own starting point Test values in the second table

Budget 2027 shows headline surpluses, but they depend on corporation tax that the Department of Finance says may be transitory. Without it the State would run a deficit of of GNI* in 2027.

The second table shows total debt if a share of that tax were lost for good, from year 1 of unity.

7h. Bounds on behaviour Test values

The paper assumes people do not change what they do when welfare rises to Republic levels. This test takes away receipts as some people work less, and adds benefit claimants over 25 years.

8. Against published estimates

The table sets the model beside FitzGerald and Morgenroth (2024) and Doyle and UUEPC (2025), using the same items where it can. Published figures are rebased to 2023 euros in the project's meta-analysis.

The model matches Doyle exactly and comes within €1 billion of FitzGerald and Morgenroth's full parity case. The base rows differ mainly because of the data year and because the model's pension and debt interest figures are larger.

9. Limits

The pay and welfare gaps come from 2019 data, which is why the model reports four cases. A benefit-by-benefit costing through a tax and benefit model is needed to settle where the true figure lies.

Pensions follow a simple linear schedule rather than an actuarial valuation, and UK debt is treated as 25 years of interest with no principal. Growth is an assumption rather than a result, and the gains from unity that reach households and firms are left out.

The Northern Ireland figures may change when the UK statistics office publishes corrected accounts. The test values in part 7 are chosen settings rather than sourced estimates.
